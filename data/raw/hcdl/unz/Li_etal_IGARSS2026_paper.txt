Abstract— This study introduces Hawaii Cropland Data 
Layers (HCDL) V2.0, the first operational crop mapping product 
leveraging Google DeepMind’s AlphaEarth Foundations Satellite 
Embedding dataset. The workflow replaces earlier multi-sensor 
image composites with 64 -dimensional satellite embeddings to 
generate annual 10-meter resolution cropland maps for Hawaii in 
2023 and 2024. The approach addresses challenges posed by 
persistent cloud cover, fragmented agricultural landscapes,  and 
limited ground -reference data, whi le improving classification 
accuracy and consistency across 27 land -cover classes. Using 
Random Forest classifiers and a mixed sampling strategy, the 
embedding-based method achieved superior performance 
compared to previous composite -based models, with mac ro-
average F1 -score gains and reduced variability across classes. 
HCDL V2.0 provides a scalable, analysis -ready solution for 
agricultural monitoring, land-use planning, and disaster resilience 
in tropical regions. The datasets are publicly available and 
visualized through the AgriWatch web application, with HCDL 
2025 currently under development.  
Keywords— Hawaii, Cropland Data Layers, Satellite Embedding 
dataset, Random Forest, Google Earth Engine, 10-meter resolution, 
AlphaEarth Foundations 
I. INTRODUCTION  
Hawaii’s agroecosystems are highly diverse, characterized 
by small farms, fragmented parcels, and varied crop types and 
management practices. Accurate, up -to-date agricultural land -
cover information is essential for improving productivity, 
sustainability, ensuring food security, and resilience to extreme 
weather events and natural disasters. The Hawaii Agricultural 
Land Use Baseline (HALUB) [1], provided by the Hawaii 
Department of Agriculture, serves as a key crop -specific land-
use dataset and offers valuable information for regional 
assessments; however, its manual aerial image digitization 
process and five -year update cycle limit its ability to capture 
rapidly changing agricultural conditions. To address this gap, 
USDA’s National Agricultural Statistics Service (NASS), in 
collaboration with the University of Hawaii at Manoa, initiated 
the development of annual 10-meter resolution Hawaii Cropland 
Data Layers (HCDL) [2][3]. 
Crop mapping in tropical regions such as Hawaii faces 
persistent challenges, particularly cloud cover, which obscures 
optical satellite imagery and complicates consistent data 
acquisition. In HCDL V1.x [3], this issue was mitigated using 
time-series 10 -day median composites of Normalized 
Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and surface reflectance 
derived from Sentinel –Landsat mult i-sensor data. These 
composites preserved crop phenology across the growing season 
but required extensive preprocessing and still struggled with 
fragmented cropping patterns and limited ground -reference 
data. 
In September 2025, Google released the first annual global 
Satellite Embedding dataset, generated by DeepMind’s 
AlphaEarth Foundations (AEF) model [ 4]. This dataset 
represents a major advancement in geospatial AI, integrating 
multiple Earth observation modalities—including optical, radar, 
LiDAR, and ancillary datasets —into a unified 64 -dimensional 
embedding space. Each embedding vector encodes temporal  
trajectories and spatial context for every 10 -meter pixel, 
capturing both spectral and semantic relationships across diverse 
landscapes. Unlike traditional spectral composites, embeddings 
are designed for generalization, enabling robust classification in 
regions with sparse labeled data [5]. 
For Hawaii, where persistent cloud cover and limited 
ground-reference samples have historically constrained crop 
mapping, this capability offers a transformative advantage. In 
this paper, we evaluate the effectiveness of AEF embeddings for 
crop-type classification and present a new operational workflow 
that replaces multi -sensor composites with embeddings to 
produce Hawaii Cropland Data Layers (HCDL) V2.0 for 2023 
and 2024. 
II. DATA 
A. Input Data 
This study utilized the AlphaEarth Foundations (AEF) 
Satellite Embedding dataset V1.0 
(GOOGLE/SATELLITE_EMBEDDING/V1/ANNUAL) for 
cropland classification in Hawaii. At 10-meter spatial resolution, 
this dataset provides a 64 -dimensional embedding vector that  
encodes temporal trajectories of surface conditions at and 
around each pixel, derived from multiple Earth observation 
sources. Although embeddings are less interpretable than 
conventional spectral inputs and vegetation indices, they 
effectively summarize relationships across multi-source, multi-
modal observations. Figure 1 illustrates the embeddings for 
Hawaii in 2024, visualized using three axes of the embedding 
space as an RGB composite. The dataset is analysis -ready, 
eliminating pre-processing steps such as atmospheric correction, 
cloud masking, spectral transformations, and speckle filtering. 
This streamlined approach reduces data preparation complexity 
and improves classification performance.

B. Ground-reference Data 
Ground-reference data were collected by the University of 
Hawaii at Manoa through fieldwork conducted between 
December 2023 and October 2025 across Kauai, Oahu, Maui, 
Molokai, Lanai, and Hawaii (Big Island). Additional 
interpretation of satellite and aerial imagery was performed to 
label crop -specific land cover, using sources such as USDA 
NAIP 2022 60 cm CIR imagery, high -resolution Google Earth 
imagery, and PlanetScope time -series imagery from 2023 and 
2024. These datasets, combined with the HALUB 2020 dataset, 
facilitated identification of active agricultural areas and 
exclusion of fallow lands. To improve differentiation between 
agricultural and non -agricultural classes, the 2021 1 -meter 
resolution Hawaii Tree Canopy GIS Layer [6] was incorporated 
to refine training labels, particularly for distinguishing tree 
canopy from cropland and delineating “commercial forest” and 
fruit tree species polygons. 
III. METHODOLOGY 
A. Processing Units 
Localized processing often yields more accurate crop 
mapping results than statewide approaches [ 7]. In Hawaii, 
similar cropping patterns typically occur within the same 
watershed. The Watershed Boundary Dataset (WBD), 
developed by the USGS, provides hydrologic units (HUs) 
delineated strictly by hydrologic principles. At the HUC10 level, 
the major Hawa iian Islands are divided into 52 watersheds. 
Given the complexity of cropping practices and the highly 
fragmented nature of parcels, this study performed classification 
independently for each watershed rather than as a single 
statewide unit. 
B. Sampling Strategies 
Sampling strategy significantly influences machine learning 
classification performance. Random Sampling (RS), while 
common, tends to over -represent dominant classes and under -
represent smaller land-cover types. Stratified Random Sampling 
(SRS) improves rep resentation but can disproportionately 
amplify small -area classes, leading to over -classification [8]. 
This issue is pronounced in Hawaii’s fragmented cropland 
patterns. To balance representation, we employed a mixed 
strategy combining RS and SRS: within each watershed, 20,000 
samples were drawn randomly, supplemented by 1,000 stratified 
samples per land -cover type. This approach produced a more 
balanced and robust training dataset. 
C. Classification Algorithms 
HCDL aims to map major, specialty, and culturally 
significant crops grown in Hawaii, including coffee, macadamia 
nuts, banana, pineapple, citrus, taro, plumeria, dracaena, papaya, 
avocado, coconut, sugarcane, sunflower, sweet potatoes, sweet 
basil, seed crops (primarily corn), and commercial forests. Due 
to limited ground -reference data, certain tropical fruit species 
(e.g., mango, litchi, longan, cacao, dragon fruit, jackfruit, 
breadfruit, and rambutan) were grouped under “Other Exotic 
Fruits,” while crops  such as tomato, pumpkin, turmeric, 
mustard, eggplant, watermelon, and onion were grouped under 
“Misc Vegs & Fruits.” 
Previous studies compared Random Forest (RF) [ 9] and 
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) [ 10][11] for crop 
mapping in Hawaii. While CNNs excelled in segmentation for 
some vegetation types, RF achieved higher accuracy and faster 
processing when classifying multi -sensor composites [3]. 
Satellite embeddings, being unit -length vectors requiring no 
normalization, are well -suited for  tree-based classifiers [ 4]. 
Therefore, this study applied RF to classify AEF’s 64 -
dimensional embeddings using the smileRandomFores t 
implementation in Google Earth Engine, configured with 100 
trees and a bagFraction of 0.5. 

D. Model Training and Validation 
Using the mixed sampling strategy, datasets were generated 
for each watershed and split into 80% training and 20% 
validation. RF classifiers were trained per watershed and applied 
for crop -type classification. Accuracy was assessed using an 
independent testing dataset of 11,681 samples (10,000 random 
and 1,681 stratified). Metrics included Overall Accuracy (OA) 
and per -class F1 -score (F1). Due to limited ground -reference 
data in 2023, model training and validation were conducted only 
for HCDL 2024. The 2023 layer was derived by applying 2024-
trained models to 2023 embeddings. 
IV. RESULTS AND DISSCUSSION 
A. Accuracy Assessment 
Figure 2 illustrates the satellite embedding-based, 10-meter 
resolution HCDL 2024 containing 21 agricultural and 6 non -
agricultural categories generated using Google Earth Engine 
(GEE) [12]. Figure 3a shows comparison of per-class F1 scores 
for 27 categories using classification derived from two datasets: 
10-day Multi -Sensor Image Composites and AEF Satellite 
Embeddings (validation set: 11,681 samples). Results indicate 
that the satellite embedding -based crop mapping generally 
outperforms the 10 -day composi te approach, with a macro -
average improvement from 0.955 to 0.965. Gains are most 
pronounced for heterogeneous crops and developed areas (e.g., 
Coffee, Macadamia, Other Exotic Fruits, Other Crops, 
Developed), while classes with near -perfect scores (e.g., O pen 
Water, Citrus, Sugarcane) remain unchanged. Minor regressions 
occur in a few categories, notably Corn (−0.023), but overall 
trends indicate a robust advantage for AEF embeddings in multi-
class land cover mapping.

Figure 3b summarizes the distribution of per class F1 scores. 
Relative to the 10 day composites, AEF embeddings exhibit a 
higher median (0.981 vs. 0.975) and a smaller interquartile range 
(IQR) (0.058 vs. 0.083), indicating improved typical 
performance and reduced variability across classes. The lower 
whisker increases (min: 0.899 vs. 0.864), and no outliers are 
present under the 1.5×IQR rule, suggesting broad based gains 
rather than isolated improvements. Consistent with these trends, 
the share of classes with F1 ≥ 0.95 rises from 63.0% to 70.4%, 
while the number at F1 ≤ 0.90 drops from 4 to 1, reinforcing the 
conclusion that AEF delivers both higher accuracy and greater 
consistency across the validation set. 
B. Landcover Change Detection 
The 2023 –2024 HCDL layers  derived from AEF satellite 
embeddings reveal significant land -cover dynamics across the 
Hawaiian Islands, highlighting both agricultural expansion and 
stability: 
• Kauai: The Kaumakani area (Figure 4a) exhibits a clear 
shift from fallow lands to a diversified agricultural 
mosaic. The 2024 HCDL shows substantial expansion of 
productive acreage driven by new energy and food crops. 
Previously uncultivated parcels have been converted into 
sunflower fields fo r biodiesel and intensive row crops 
such as watermelon and mixed vegetables. This “fallow-
to-productive” signal underscores the region’s evolving 
role in local sustainability and food security initiatives.

• Oahu: Agricultural footprints remain relatively stable 
across the observation period, demonstrating resilience 
despite intensifying competition for land resources. 
However, this stability coexists with visible 
encroachment from urbanization and renewable e nergy 
infrastructure, particularly in the Central and Ewa plains 
(Figure 4b left) . The data highlights a spatial tension 
between persistent cultivation and the expansion of 
competing land uses, signaling potential pressure on key 
agricultural corridors. 
• Maui: In the Central Valley (Figure 4c), the 2023–2024 
HCDL layers capture a critical maturation phase. Unlike 
the clearing and planting signatures of previous years, 
recent data shows distinct canopy closure across 
approximately 13,000 acres of permanent orchards, 
primarily citrus. This marks a stable transition from 
former sugarcane monoculture to a diversified 
agricultural mosaic, where established tree crops are 
interspersed with rotational fields of watermelon and 
onion. 
• Molokai: The Hoʻolehua plains  (Figure 4d) exhibit a 
dual-anchor of agricultural stability. The seed corn area 
maintains a high -intensity footprint, with spectral 
signatures reflecting dense planting of short-stature corn 
varieties. Adjacent to these seasonal field crops, the 
papaya orchards display a resilient, permanent “Tropical 
Fruit” signature. Together, these areas form the island’s 
economic core—a mix of industrial seed production and 
premium export -fruit cultivation that has successfully 
maintained a ctive acreage despite regional drought 
pressures. 
• Hawaii Island: The Hilo and Hamakua agricultural 
corridors (Figure 4b right)  present a distinct “dual -
system” landscape where permanent tree canopies 
coexist with dynamic, open -field farming. Established 
orchards such as macadamia and lychee provide a stable 
perennial signature, while large tracts are frequently 
plowed and rotated  for high-value root crops including 
ginger, turmeric, and sweet potato. This creates a 
complex visual mosaic where rapid changes —from 
green vegetation to bare soil —represent activ e crop 
rotation rather than development or abandonment. 
 
V. SUMMARY AND FUTURE WORK 
This study demonstrates the successful integration of Google 
DeepMind’s AlphaEarth Foundations Satellite Embedding 
dataset into operational crop mapping for Hawaii, resulting in 
the first 10 -meter resolution Hawaii Cropland Data Layers 
(HCDL) V2.0 for 2023  and 2024. By replacing earlier multi-
sensor image composites with satellite embeddings, the new 
workflow significantly improves classification accuracy and 
consistency across diverse crop types, particularly in cloud -
prone and fragmented agricultural landscapes. The embedding-
based approach streamlines data preparation, reduces reliance 
on extensive ground -reference datasets, and offers a scalable 
solution for annual cropland monitoring. These advancements 
not only enhance agricultural decision -making and land-use 
planning in Hawaii but also provide a blueprint for advancing 
USDA-NASS’s 10-meter resolution nationwide Cropland Data 
Layers [7] production especially in data-limited regions.
