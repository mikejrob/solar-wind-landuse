A.

CHANGING THE BILLING DETERMINANT.

As noted above, the Spring Revenue Report reflects the recovery of the
accumulated RBA Adjustment based on a billing determinant of the estimated percentage
of bill rather than the prior method, which recovered the accumulated RBA Adjustment by
dividing the adjustment by the estimated kWh. As discussed in greater detail in the
Commission’s Decision and Order No. 38680, filed on October 31, 2022, in
Docket No. 2018-0088 (“D&O 38680”), this new billing determinant was adopted to
address concerns that continuing to use kWh as the billing determinant to recover the
RBA Adjustment could perpetuate and exacerbate concerns with certain customers being
able to bypass or avoid having to contribute (or receive in the instance of the RBA
Adjustment returning funds to customers) to the amount to be recovered from customers.
While different proposals were offered, the Companies offered its percentage of base bill
basis, which the Commission adopted in D&O 38680. 13 Further, the Commission agreed
with the Companies’ proposal that this method should be applied to all customers and not
just the customers placed on TOU rates. 14 As noted by the Commission, the adoption of
the percentage of bill method was meant to address the bypass issues 15 and also to
address concerns with inter-class revenue allocations. 16

be modifications to the new method. For instance, the Companies’ response highlights
one of the topics that was discussed in the April 11, 2023 meeting, wherein the question
was posed regarding whether using the target test year revenues from a rate proceeding
that was developed years ago might result in a higher percentage adjustment factor as
compared to using recent historical billed revenues. As noted in a comparison between
the cumulative revenues reflected on each company’s respective B2 to the target
revenues shown on each company’s respective B1, there are variances between the test
year target revenues and the recorded revenues and it is not unreasonable to expect any
such variance will increase in future years as the time (and revenues) collected will tend
to increase beyond the target revenue set in the past. Using the target revenues as the
basis for the divisor is likely to create an inherent bias towards having a larger percentage
adjustment factor, whether it is to collect or return money, since the divisor will likely be
smaller if target revenues are used.

The Consumer Advocate offers the following

thoughts. The Companies’ response to informal CA-IR-17c.1. states that it “is difficult to
assess which of these two RBA Divisor calculation methodologies (using the test year
revenues and forecasted surcharges versus using historical billed revenues) would
minimize the difference between what the Companies collect and target revenue and
thereby result in a lower RBA balance.” The Consumer Advocate offers that based on
Attachment 2, using recent recorded billed revenues instead of the test year target
revenues resulted in a lower bill impact in every instance, which is to be expected since
recent billed

revenues

are greater than

the

target

revenues.

Thus, the

Consumer Advocate offers, barring other evidence that suggests otherwise, allowing the
test year target revenues to be used as the basis for the RBA divisor will lead to

Trans. No. 23-01

8

increasingly greater variances between what is collected and what should be collected,
and the Commission should strongly consider using recent, historical billed revenues as
the basis for the RBA divisor. It is likely that the Companies will offer that a true-up will
occur under either method but the Consumer Advocate offers that it is in the best interest
of customers to minimize early over-collection from customers even if the money will be
returned later. Minimizing such over-collection provides value to the customers in terms
of not losing the time value of money.
Furthermore, the Consumer Advocate offers that the adoption of the percentage
of bill method will partially address concerns with subsidies, which was recognized in
D&O 38680.

That is, using the legacy kWh approach, the RBA Adjustment was

recovered (or returned) across all kWh sales regardless of where the revenue undercollection or over-collection occurred. Thus, the likelihood of cross-class subsidies was
much greater where, for example, if there were revenue shortfalls from the commercial
and industrial classes during COVID, all other things being held equal, those revenue
shortfalls would be recovered through all kWh, including kWh sales to residential
customers. Another example would be that for any revenue shortfalls due to distributed
energy resource (“DER”) systems offsetting customers use, all other things being held
equal, those revenue shortfalls would be recovered through all kWh, including kWh sales
to non-participating customers across all classes; thus, since the majority of DER systems
are in the residential customer class, any RBA Adjustment would have been recovered
from all kWh, including from non-participants as well as across all customer classes but
DER participants may not contribute as much to the recovery due to their system reducing
the kWh for participating customers. Thus, the new method should at least partially

Trans. No. 23-01

9

address concerns with both inter-class and intra-class subsidies and reduce the
possibility of bypassing the RBA. However, it should be clear that the new method will
mitigate cross-class subsidies due to the bypass opportunities being decreased but the
new method will not address all concerns with cross-class subsidies.
As the results of the comparison show, the adoption of the percentage of bill
method appears to be resulting in reallocating a portion of the amount to be recovered,
which may be partially addressing past inter-class or cross-class subsidies.

The

Consumer Advocate offers, however, further evaluation of the need for additional
modifications to the percentage of bill method may be necessary to further mitigate interclass or cross-class subsidies as well as to evaluate the impact of intra-class subsidies
(e.g., non-participants, especially vulnerable, fixed income, or LMI, customers bearing a
disproportionate share of any RBA Adjustment collection). Thus, the Consumer Advocate
stresses the importance of evaluating and assessing the impact of this and other rate
design changes, such as those anticipated in the ARD time of use rollout and the potential
impacts on customers.
The new method could, however, lead to a likelihood of greater variances between
the RBA revenue adjustment to be collected and the revenues actually collected through
the RBA surcharge. There have always been variances between an individual customer’s
bill and the average bill for that customer class and the average bills between classes
have also varied. Furthermore, those variances have likely increased over time due to
the changes in customers within the class. Since the new method uses cumulative
adjusted revenues as the divisor to determine the adjustment factor, this may also result
in subsidies.

That is, the legacy method, which relied on kWh, would favor those

Trans. No. 23-01

10

customers who was able to reduce their kWh through DERs, energy efficiency, or
changes in customer behavior when there was a revenue shortfall since they would pay
less towards that shortfall. The new method mitigates the bypass that could occur but
might expose residential customers since the commercial and industrial customers have
historically generated more revenues and variances in those revenues would have likely
have a greater impact on the RBA divisor and resulting RBA percentage that will be used.
For that reason, the Consumer Advocate has offered in Docket No. 2019-0323, and offers
again in this forum, that it is likely necessary to analyze revenue shortfalls and overcollections by class in the future to evaluate whether the inter-class subsidies are
significant to warrant urgent actions.
In addition, the Consumer Advocate offers that customer outreach to provide
information to customers about the pending change and the potential impacts on their
bills should be made available as soon as possible, especially since the requested
June 1 effective date is rapidly approaching.

Given the change, there are certain

customers who will likely see a relative increase in their bills because they may have
contributed nothing to very little towards RBA charges due to no or low net kWh historical
usage and the Companies recognize that there are customers who have not previously
received an RBA rate adjustment charge in the past in their response to informal
CA--IR--15. The Companies’ outreach plan appears to consist of “preparing talking points
on the revised RBA adjustment to provide their customer call center in advance of
June 1, 2023…” Recognizing that there is not much time before June 1, 2023, the
Consumer Advocate still offers that there should have been more effort and efforts that
can still be taken by the Companies to develop and offer customer outreach information

Trans. No. 23-01

11

in advance of the effective date so that customers could have received at least some
basic information prior to the June 1, 2023 effective date to prepare for the possibility of
higher electric bills.
Thus, as it relates to the new billing method, the Consumer Advocate
recommends:
-

The Companies should use recent billed revenues as the basis for the RBA
divisor instead of the test year target revenues.

-

The Commission should require additional analysis to evaluate the revenue
shortfall or overcollection by customer class to support an informed decision
whether additional urgent actions may be required to mitigate interclass
subsidies that the new billing method may exacerbate.

-

Even though it might be too late to provide greater warning of the impending
change in the billing method for collecting the RBA adjustment, the
Companies should inform the Commission and Consumer Advocate if there
is a significant number of calls and/or complaints related to the change so
that a future decision could be made whether additional measures might be
reasonable.

During the review of the supporting details, the Consumer Advocate noted that
there were instances of where the Companies are claiming a zero interval for quick

having different incentives rewarding the same activity and how such a practice could
result in customers paying higher bills as well as create confusion and challenges about
measuring the efficacy of the individual PIMs.
