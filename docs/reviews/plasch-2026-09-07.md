Hi Mike,

I reviewed the section of your report that discusses the availability of land for solar farms. Here are my quick comments, which I hope are helpful.

I think the following areas should be removed as potential locations for solar farms:

* James Campbell National Wildlife Refuge
* Kualoa Regional Park
* Patsy T Mink Central Oahu Regional Park
* Kualoa Ranch (solar farms are unlikely to be the most profitable use of the land)
* Waikāne Valley (unexploded ordnance from WWII)
* Waiāhole Valley (leased long-term as farm lots but used mostly for homes)
* Upper parts of the Waiʻanae Mountains (very rough terrain, small developable areas, very difficult access, far from the grid).

Additionally, some areas are de facto public parks, such as the old Haleʻiwa airport site, a complex of mountain-bike trails on land abutting Pūpūkea, and commercial motocross trails to the north. With a better map (larger with identifying features such as roads), I suspect that I could identify other areas unsuitable for solar farms.

Should your analysis specify a minimum size for a utility solar farm (e.g., 6 MW or about 30 acres based on 5 acres/MW)? Many farm parcels are too small for utility solar farms. Also, your map includes some areas that already host large solar farms. Should they be identified on your map?

I think agrivoltaic is an excellent idea, particularly when sheep are employed to manage vegetation. This arrangement allows farmers to earn income from two sources: vegetation-removal services and lamb sales. The solar companies would gain politically if they were to help fund a slaughterhouse for lambs. For agrivoltaic integrated with crop farming, I would note that the panels are generally elevated and spaced to ensure sunlight reaches the crops. This incurs higher development costs, increases O&M costs if farm-related dust must be washed off the panels, and requires more land. It may be prudent to acknowledge this.

The State has made a major commitment to agriculture on the North Shore by purchasing Galbraith and Dole farmland, acquiring the Wahiawā Reservoir, and funding the Central O'ahu Agriculture and Food Hub. Consequently, I believe the State will be reluctant to allow solar farms on their farmland for those fields that can be irrigated. Further, the North Shore Neighborhood Board adapted a policy strongly opposing utility solar farms on agricultural land. Again, it may be prudent to acknowledge this.

Regarding seed corn, the common practice is to rotate the planted and buffer fields. Since buffer fields are not fixed, solar farms may not be feasible within these temporary buffer fields.

Regarding rents, solar farms pay far higher rents than ranchers pay for pastureland. However, rents for irrigated cropland can be competitive with solar-farm rents, and potentially higher for small farm lots if homes are permitted. Additionally, some farmers are financially well-off and are farming primarily for the lifestyle. Consequently, many of these farmers may not be receptive to their land being used for solar farms. This is particularly true for many of the small farms located in the Koʻolauloa and North Shore Districts.

I highly recommend securing funding to conduct an in-depth study of the land on Oʻahu that could realistically be available for utility solar farms. This study should be conducted jointly with one or more of our major planning firms, such as G70, PBR, HHF, etc. It would be beneficial to check which firms recently worked on community plans or are currently working on them. Some of the senior planners can assist with access to major landowners; federal, state, and county agencies; neighborhood boards; and more. They are knowledgeable about State and County land-use policies and community plans, and generally have a good sense of what is feasible. Such a study is clearly needed, and you would become the expert on the issue.

Aloha,

Bruce
